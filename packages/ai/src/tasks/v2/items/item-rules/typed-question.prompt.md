- `question`: one direct instruction that says what to answer and how much: one word, a number, or one or two sentences ("In one or two sentences, say why a metal spoon feels colder than a wooden one in the same room."). It asks one thing, or two linked parts at most (a thing and what it does, an effect and its reason), never several questions in one. The learner can tell from it exactly what a right answer states: never a vague "explain in your own words" or "what do you think about it", and never an angle, a scope or a detail they would have to guess.
- `keyPoints`: 1 to 3 ideas a right answer states, one for each part `question` asks for, each one idea a grader can check on its own (never two joined by "and"). A key point the question doesn't ask for (a detail, a second reason, a place, a term) is a hidden expectation the learner can't meet. Write them so an answer in the learner's own words meets them: a key point never asks for a term's name ("é transporte ativo", "difusão facilitada") unless the question asks the learner to name it, and states the idea instead ("a saída gasta ATP e vai contra o gradiente"). A question that asks to predict and explain asks for the effect and its reason, not for the process's classification or a detail the reason doesn't need. A question that asks what to do, suggest or check (an improvement, a check, a strategy, a way to decide) takes any sound answer: each key point says what every sound answer achieves, with ways to do it only as examples ("finds out why the seedlings wilt before changing anything, for example by checking how wet the soil is or how much sun they get"; "checks only what decides between the two options, briefly, instead of redoing everything"), never one technique among several ("compares the procedures from the step where they diverge", "eliminates the options that contradict the text") that a sound answer may replace with another.
