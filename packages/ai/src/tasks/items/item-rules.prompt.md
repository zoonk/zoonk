# What makes a good question

- It makes the learner use the skill in a situation: apply, predict, compare, diagnose, calculate or explain. Never ask to recall a definition word for word.
- Each question stands alone. A learner who never saw a lesson, but has the skill, can answer it.
- It never gives its answer away: the learner does the skill's work. The context and the question hold only what the learner needs to start, and no option shows that work (see the formats).
- A reading or interpretation question makes the learner understand the text (an inference, the main idea, how two parts relate, a detail asked in other words), never find a word, number or phrase the text states in the question's own words ("The buses leave every 30 minutes." then "How often do the buses leave?").
- Situations are concrete and realistic.{{FIELD_RULE}}
- Vary the situations and what each question asks, and spread the difficulty (`easy`, `medium`, `hard`) across the set.
- Fit `LEVEL`: overview and beginner questions use everyday words and one step; intermediate combine two ideas; advanced ones need judgment or several steps. For an exam, its own difficulty comes first (see "Exam style").
- Be correct. If a fact depends on a country, a date or an edition, name it in the question. Never invent statistics, laws or quotes.
- All learner-facing text is in `LANGUAGE`{{LANGUAGE_GOAL_EXCEPTION}}. Speak to the learner as "you" in reasons and feedback.
- Situations use the money and everyday life in `LOCAL_CONTEXT`, unless `EXAM` or the skill sets another place (SAT questions stay American even in Portuguese). People and towns come from `CAST`, in its order: each question of a set has its own person and, when its situation needs a town, its own town, never the same name or city twice. Other questions are written at the same time with other casts, so never swap in the names every writer reaches for. A real person, company or city stays when the skill is about it.

# Exam style

When `EXAM` is given, write original questions that look, read and score like that exam's real ones, following `EXAM_STYLE`: its structure (support text, command, number of options), tone, length and typical traps.

They are as hard as the exam's recent papers for the candidates it admits or selects, never a textbook exercise on the same topic: the same number of reasoning steps, the same number of ideas combined in one question, the same close reading of the support text and the same traps. A selective exam's questions chain several ideas and steps (a JEE Advanced problem combines two or three concepts and carries a result from one step into the next; a Cebraspe statement turns on one exact condition of a rule), so even its `easy` questions are its own easier ones, well above a routine exercise. `LEVEL` and the difficulty only place a question within the exam's range, never below it.

`PAST_QUESTIONS`, when given, are real questions from the exam's papers, as printed: match their difficulty, length and number of steps. Never copy or lightly edit a real past question, and never reuse its situation. When `EXAM` is "none", write clear everyday questions.

# Text, tables, charts, timelines and pictures

A question whose words point at something to see shows it, and never describes it in words instead.

- Write plain sentences. When a question needs data a learner reads in rows and columns, put it in `context` as a GFM pipe table: a header row, a delimiter row (`---:` under columns of numbers), one row per line, and a blank line before and after it. Never put a table in `question` or an option.
{{PICTURES}}
- Never draw a chart or diagram with characters (bars of █, an axis of `|`).
- Use no other Markdown, except **bold** or _italics_ on a word that needs it. To quote a word, a caption or a sentence, put it in _italics_, never between « » or << >>.
- Write your own words (the question, statements, options, reasons, rubric, key points and sample answers) without em dashes (—): use a comma, a colon, parentheses or a new sentence ("Competência 1: domínio da modalidade escrita formal"). A text you quote in `context` (an excerpt, a transcript, a poem) keeps its own punctuation, exactly as written.

# Final check

Before answering, make sure every question has exactly one defensible correct answer{{#severalPicks}} (or set of correct options, when the learner picks several){{/severalPicks}}, the answers are right, no option shows the computed result or the reasoning that picks it, no two questions test the same thing in the same way, and all learner-facing text is in `LANGUAGE`{{LANGUAGE_GOAL_CHECK}}.
