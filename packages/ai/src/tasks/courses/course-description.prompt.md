Write a short, inviting course description in LANGUAGE. Use one or two plain sentences that show the learner what they will be able to explain or do. Normally 25–45 words are enough. Name a concrete, relatable outcome instead of listing technical subfields or abstract terminology.

Match FORMAT:

- Core: convey the subject's useful promise. The complete curriculum can go deeper, but its description should be easy for a beginner to read. Do not promise a job or list employers/careers as boilerplate.
- Language: describe everyday communication and room to progress from A1 to C2. Avoid implying learners must start at A1.
- Question: promise a brief answer to the actual question, with one useful observation or example. Do not advertise a comprehensive subject, mastery, a career, or a technical syllabus.
- Personalized: describe the stated practical outcome without inventing a broader curriculum.

Use familiar words and direct, natural language. It is fine to say learn, understand, or explore when natural; avoid canned openings, hype and repeated promises. Named tools can appear when they are the actual subject. Do not define an unfamiliar term using more unfamiliar terms.

Accuracy comes before a tidy story. Distinguish observations from proposed explanations when a cause is disputed or incompletely understood. Do not claim an open scientific question is settled. For example, the Moon illusion is a real change in perceived size; distance cues are a proposed explanation, not a proven complete cause. A useful description can invite the learner to compare what their eyes see with a simple measurement without listing competing theories.

Examples:

- Computer Science: "See how computers turn simple instructions into the tools you use every day. Build a clear picture of how software works, then go deeper into solving problems and designing reliable systems."
- Why the Moon Looks Bigger Near the Horizon: "See why the Moon can look huge near the horizon even when its measured size barely changes. Try a simple comparison and separate what we know from what scientists are still working out."
- German: "Build the German you need for conversations, reading, and everyday life. Start at your level and progress from A1 to C2."
